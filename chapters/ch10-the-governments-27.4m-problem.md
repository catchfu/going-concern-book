# Chapter 10 — The Government's $27.4M Problem

*Status: draft v0.2*

The Commonwealth of Australia held $27.4m in active KPMG audit contracts when the scandal broke. That number — specific, unglamorous, administrative — became the reference point for a political reckoning that went far beyond one firm. The question the government faced was not whether KPMG had done wrong; it was whether the public sector, which had become one of the big four's most reliable customers, could keep doing business with a firm that had betrayed the trust on which all audit work depends.

The $27.4m was the headline, but it was not the whole story. KPMG held $653m in government contracts in total — federal, state, and territory work ranging from IT to consulting to advisory services. The audit contracts were the moral core of the relationship: the government, like any client, had appointed KPMG to verify the accounts of public entities, and KPMG had been caught using its access to confidential material for its own commercial gain. If the government could not trust its auditor, who could?

## The freeze debate

Within days of the scandal breaking, the politics of the government's exposure exploded. The Greens called for a complete ban on KPMG government work. Senator Barbara Pocock and others argued that a firm under investigation for exactly the kind of conduct that should disqualify it from public contracts could not be permitted to keep bidding. "KPMG must be banned from all government work, say Greens as ASIC investigates," reported Region Canberra.

The government's response was a half-measure that satisfied no one. KPMG would not bid for new federal work until the end of September 2026 — a three-month suspension presented as voluntary, but understood universally as a de facto ban. The Australian's verdict was blunt: "KPMG government work ban a 'slap on the wrist'." The firm retained its $653m in existing contracts. The suspension, the criticism ran, allowed KPMG to keep the revenue it had already won while only limiting its future growth — the same calculus that had protected the partnership in every previous scandal.

## NSW and Victoria

The states moved faster and further than the Commonwealth. NSW — where the finance minister, Courtney Houssos, had been demanding accountability — ordered its agencies to compile the full list of KPMG contracts, and made clear that new work would face scrutiny. NSW's demand for a complete contract list was the first step toward the kind of transparency the federal government had declined to impose.

Victoria went further still. "KPMG banned from Victorian government work with millions at stake," reported the AFR. The Victorian ban — a genuine prohibition rather than a voluntary pause — was the first concrete exclusion of KPMG from a major jurisdiction. "Major setback for KPMG in Australia: Confidential data scandal sparks Victorian government ban," ran the Gulf News headline. The Victorian decision demonstrated what the federal response had avoided: a state prepared to treat the KPMG scandal as disqualifying conduct rather than as a reputational inconvenience.

## The wider reckoning

The government's problem was not only KPMG. The scandal forced a reckoning with the public sector's broader reliance on the big four — the same firms that had, between them, been responsible for the PwC tax leak, the consulting overcharging scandals, and now the KPMG breach. Treasury was ordered to investigate the "policy problems" with the big four's dominance of government work. The phrase in The Statesman's reporting — "Two CEOs down, $270m at risk: Australia might ban KPMG after second Big Four scandal in three years" — captured the accumulating weight of the profession's failures.

The Commonwealth's decision to refer KPMG to the National Anti-Corruption Commission (NACC) added a new dimension. A firm under investigation by the corporate regulator and now referred to the anti-corruption watchdog had crossed a threshold: this was no longer a professional-misconduct matter but a question of integrity in government procurement. The referral did not impose a penalty, but it signalled that the government regarded the conduct as sufficiently serious to warrant the attention of the body created to investigate corruption.

By August, the reckoning had reached the government's own benches: The Nightly reported a push within Labor to stop outsourcing amid the KPMG scandal — a debate over whether the public sector's reliance on the consulting firms had outlived its usefulness.

## The $27.4m and the $653m

The arithmetic of the affair was never comfortable. The $27.4m in active audit contracts was small relative to the $653m total, but the audit contracts were the symbol. A government that kept its auditor after the auditor was caught leaking client information was a government that had abandoned the principle the audit function exists to protect. The states understood this; the Commonwealth, at least initially, did not.

The question that remains — and that this chapter will revisit as the story develops — is whether the federal freeze is a genuine reform or a calculated pause. KPMG's suspension ends in September 2026. Whether it returns to federal work, on what terms, and whether the states' harder lines survive the political cycle, will determine whether the government's $27.4m problem was a moment of accountability or another slap on the wrist.
