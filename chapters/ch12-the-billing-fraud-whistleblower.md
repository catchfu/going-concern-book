# Chapter 12 — The Billing Fraud Whistleblower

*Status: draft v0.2*

The KPMG affair had two whistleblowers, and this book has so far told only one story. The first — the former audit director whose complaint exposed the Lendlease breach — triggered the resignations, the ASIC probe, and the political crisis. The second was a whistleblower with a different allegation entirely: not about confidential documents, but about money. The claim was that KPMG partners had billed clients for work that had not been done — inflated hours, fictional hours, charged to an insurance client in a pattern that the whistleblower described as endemic.

The two allegations, delivered at almost the same moment, collided in the phrase Michael West Media used for one of its earliest stories on the affair: "'Blows my mind': KPMG's whistleblower issues collide." The firm was simultaneously accused of breaching client confidentiality and of defrauding clients. Each allegation, on its own, would have been a serious scandal. Together, they exposed the two ways the big four's economics corrupt their work: selling access to confidential material for commercial advantage, and billing for hours that were never worked.

## The allegation

The billing fraud allegation, as it has been reported, concerns an insurance client. The whistleblower alleged that KPMG partners inflated hours worked on the client's engagement, that some hours recorded were entirely fictional, and that the billing practices were so established that they amounted to a systemic pattern rather than isolated cases. The allegation, made under conditions of anonymity, went to the heart of how KPMG made its money.

The audit economics beneath the scandal are straightforward and unforgiving. Audit fees are fixed or declining; the hours that produce them are heavily discounted; and the consulting and advisory work that fills the gap is billed by the hour. In that economics, there is relentless pressure to show billable hours — pressure that, the whistleblower alleged, produced invoices for work that did not happen. The billing allegations, if true, were not a departure from the firm's practices but a product of them.

## The 2023 precursor

The allegations echoed a scandal that had surfaced two years earlier. In August 2023, the ABC reported that KPMG had overcharged the Defence department while raking in billions of dollars, according to whistleblowers. The Defence overcharging claims concerned work on Defence's major IT and consulting engagements — work that the ABC's reporting described as billed at rates and volumes that did not reflect the services delivered.

The Defence scandal had been a warning. It had produced a parliamentary inquiry, a review, and a set of promises about how the firm would improve its billing practices. And yet, by the time the second whistleblower came forward in 2026, the allegation was not that the firm had failed to reform; it was that the same practices were continuing — and that they were not confined to one government client but extended into the private sector, into the insurance client whose invoices the whistleblower described.

## The intersection with the audit scandal

The billing allegations intersected with the Lendlease scandal in a way that made both worse. The firm accused of misusing confidential documents for commercial advantage was the same firm accused of billing for work not done. The two allegations together painted a picture of a partnership whose culture valued revenue above integrity in every dimension: in winning work (the leaked documents), in charging for work (the inflated hours), and in responding to the people who raised concerns (the dismissed and surveilled whistleblower).

The parliamentary committee took an interest in the billing claims, though the primary focus of the public hearings remained the Lendlease breach and the surveillance. The billing allegations were harder to investigate publicly — they rested on client invoices and engagement files that the firm controlled and would not produce. The committee's demand for documents, and the firm's refusal on privilege grounds, meant that the billing story remained, at the time of writing, largely unexamined in public.

## Why the billing story matters

The billing allegations matter for reasons beyond the specific claim. They explain the incentive structure that produced the scandal. A firm whose partners are remunerated on revenue and billable hours will be a firm under constant pressure to win work by any means and to bill for work in any manner. The Lendlease breach was the extreme product of the first pressure; the billing allegations were the product of the second. The two were not coincidental scandals but symptoms of the same economics.

They also matter because they test the limited liability shield described in Chapter 11. Billing clients for work not performed is not professional negligence; it is, if proven, fraud. And the professional standards caps that protect partners from negligence liability were never designed to protect fraud. If the billing allegations are proven, the shield may not apply — and the partners involved could face personal liability, criminal consequences, or both.

## August: the denial undercut

The August hearing did not centre the billing allegations, but it did undercut the firm's prior denials in a way that left the claims very much alive. The Australian's 14 August coverage noted that "most of those allegations have since proven true" against KPMG over the audit whistleblower — language that cast a pall over the firm's insistence that the billing claims were similarly unsubstantiated. Deputy counsel James McClelland's admission that his investigation had been "fundamentally undermined" by partners who were "misleading, if not directly deceptive" — testimony given as the committee simultaneously examined a second, tax-division settlement disclosed as "larger than $500,000" — confirmed that the firm's first reflexive response to each complaint had been to investigate the complainant rather than the complaint. Whether the billing files are ever produced remains an open question; the posture of the defence does not.

## The state of the evidence

At the time of writing, the billing fraud allegations remain exactly that: allegations. No public finding has been made. The whistleblower's account has been reported, the firm has denied wrongdoing, and the documents that would resolve the matter are in the firm's hands. This chapter records the allegation as it stands, and flags it as one of the most consequential open questions of the entire affair — because if the billing claims are proven, the KPMG scandal is not only a story about confidentiality and surveillance, but a story about the criminal economics of the big four.
