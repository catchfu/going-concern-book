# Chapter 7 — The Chairman's Conflict

*Status: draft v0.2*

Martin Sheppard was, in the early weeks of 2026, the picture of a successful professional: chairman of KPMG Australia, chairman of KPMG Asia Pacific, a career partner with a record of service to the firm. By the end of June he had resigned, the first chairman of a major audit firm to be driven from office by a scandal that he had, in the view of his critics, spent a month trying to survive rather than resolve.

The question at the centre of the chairman's conflict is not what Sheppard did — he was not accused of the Lendlease breach itself — but what he knew, what he disclosed, and how he responded once the whistleblower's allegations became public. The answers, as they emerged, were fatal.

## The war room

When the scandal broke, Sheppard presided over what reporting described as the firm's "war room" — the crisis-response operation KPMG assembled to manage the fallout. The war room's purpose was to control the narrative: to keep the committee away from the internal investigation files, to frame the resignations as accountability, and to preserve what could be preserved of the firm's client relationships. It was, in the language of crisis management, a professional operation. But the war room's existence also revealed the chairman's instinct: to treat the whistleblower's allegations as a communications problem rather than as a governance crisis requiring radical disclosure.

## The disclosure question

The conflict at the heart of the chairman's position was one of disclosure. Sheppard had been chairman when the whistleblower's complaint was investigated and dismissed. He had presided over the board that had overruled the conduct panel in the Hoggett decision. The question the parliamentary committee pursued was whether Sheppard had disclosed to the board, and to clients, the full extent of what he knew about the Lendlease breach and the surveillance of the whistleblower.

The AFR's reporting on "KPMG chairman Martin Sheppard and problems of disclosure" framed the issue precisely. A chairman whose own disclosure obligations are in question cannot credibly lead a firm whose audit opinions depend on disclosure. The firm's own independent directors — Mike Baird and Jane Hemstritch — had been appointed, in part, to provide exactly the oversight that the chairman's conduct now placed in doubt.

## "I rued trust": Mike Baird turns on the chairman

The turning point was the testimony of Mike Baird. Baird, the former NSW Premier who had joined the board as an independent director, gave evidence to the committee in mid-June. His account was remarkable less for what it revealed about the firm than for what it revealed about the board: the independent directors had been kept at arm's length, their attempts to understand the scale of the problem frustrated, and their trust in the chairman broken.

"I resigned... I rued trust," Baird said — a sentence that became the epigraph for the entire affair. The Capital Brief headline captured the dynamic: "Mike Baird turns up the heat on KPMG chair." A former premier, a man of political stature, testifying that the firm's response to the whistleblower had been too slow, and that the chairman had lost the confidence of the very board he was meant to lead.

## Senate scrutiny in camera

The committee's demand for the firm's internal documents collided with the firm's assertion of legal professional privilege. The battle was fought partly in public and partly in camera — in closed session, where the committee examined the privilege claims. The firm and its lawyers, Allens, argued that the investigation files were privileged and need not be produced. The committee, and Senator Deborah O'Neill in particular, treated the refusal as evidence that the firm had something to hide.

The in camera hearings created a paradox that would recur throughout the affair: the more KPMG asserted privilege, the more it confirmed the committee's suspicion that the internal processes had protected the firm rather than held it to account. The chairman's office — the war room — was the author of the privilege strategy. It was a strategy that would end his chairmanship.

## The resignation

Sheppard resigned on 23 June 2026, less than a month after the scandal broke and days after Baird's testimony. The resignation was accompanied by the departure of senior partners and an overhaul of the firm's leadership. In his statement, Sheppard acknowledged the damage to the firm and to the profession, but the resignation itself was read as the last act of the war room: an attempt to draw a line under the crisis by sacrificing the figurehead.

The Capital Brief headline — "KPMG Australia chair Martin Sheppard resigns, leadership overhauled amid audit leaks scandal" — was followed by the broader judgment of the press. "KPMG chairman's departure is just one of a thousand cuts," wrote the SMH. "Professional Life: How Sheppard steered KPMG into calamity," said the AFR. The verdict of the reporting was that Sheppard had not caused the breach but had failed the test of leadership: the test of disclosure, of candour, and of recognising that a chairman's first loyalty is to the truth, not to the firm's survival.

## The successor's stumble

Sheppard's successor was Michael Ebeid, whose elevation the AFR reported on 12 August 2026 alongside the news that Macquarie and Westpac would front the coming hearing. But Ebeid's chairmanship began under fire. Ahead of the hearing, it emerged that he had sent Senator Deborah O'Neill a dismissive email labelling the whistleblower's allegations "false"; as the hearing approached he apologised, calling the email — in Capital Brief's account — "naive, embarrassing, wrong." The apology ran in The Australian, the SMH, Michael West Media and Accountants Daily, and the AFR framed the new chairman's position as "Michael Ebeid's last-ditch effort to save KPMG, and himself." The chairman's conflict, it turned out, had not ended with Sheppard's departure. It had been inherited.

The firm's global leadership, meanwhile, stayed away altogether: the AFR reported on 13 August that KPMG's global bosses had snubbed O'Neill's inquiry. The firm whose Australian chairman had been forced out could not bring its international leadership to face the committee at all.

## The Sheppard files

The chairman's conflict did not close with his resignation; it was carried into the document war. By late September, Accounting Times reported that KPMG still claimed privilege over 74 documents — the internal record that would show what the chairman knew about the breach, the surveillance, and the overruled conduct panel remaining behind the privilege wall. The SMH's September profile of the committee's pursuit — "The political foes chasing down KPMG's 'wolves' have not finished yet" — framed the fight as personal: O'Neill and Pocock against a firm that had, in their telling, treated the parliament as another counterparty to be managed.

The Macquarie dimension added a postscript. The Australian reported in October that "Macquarie backs directors as KPMG audit contact gets personal" — the bank defending its directors' meetings with KPMG figures during the audit tender — after the AFR had reported that a Macquarie director had "repeatedly met KPMG figures during the audit bid" and Accounting Times that Macquarie had given the director a "clean chit." The chairman's conflict had metastasised into a governance question about the clients themselves: who at Macquarie knew what, and whether the bank's near-$100m audit appointment had been conducted at the distance audit independence requires.

## The question that remains

Sheppard's resignation answered the question of his tenure, but not the question of his conduct. Did he know about the surveillance of the whistleblower before the committee did? Did he direct the overruling of the conduct panel in the Hoggett decision? Was the war room's strategy — the privilege fight, the in camera hearings — his strategy? These questions now rest with the parliamentary committee, with ASIC, and, potentially, with the courts. They are the open questions of a scandal that consumed its own chairman before it was done with the firm.
