# Chapter 9 — ASIC Enters

*Status: draft v0.2*

For the first weeks after the whistleblower's allegations became public, the corporate regulator was a spectator. ASIC — the Australian Securities and Investments Commission, the country's corporate and markets regulator — watched the resignations, the client exodus, and the parliamentary hearings from the sidelines. Then, in the first week of June 2026, it moved. ASIC announced a formal investigation into KPMG Australia's partners over the audit leak scandal, and with that announcement, the affair ceased to be a governance crisis and became a regulatory one.

The question this chapter examines is what ASIC could actually do. For an institution that has spent years being criticised — including by its own chair, and by the profession it regulates — as a regulator that "gutted its own audit teams" and then wondered why no one was watching the auditors, the KPMG investigation was a moment of truth.

## The probe begins

Reuters reported on 5 June 2026 that KPMG Australia faced a probe by the corporate regulator as the audit leak scandal fallout widened. The scope, as it emerged, was broad: ASIC would examine the conduct of the partners involved in the Lendlease breach, the firm's handling of the whistleblower's complaint, and — in a widening that surprised many — the firm's use of confidential Optus information and the surveillance of the whistleblower's laptop.

The investigation was not limited to KPMG. ASIC also opened a wider review into how the four big audit firms handle internal complaints. The regulator's logic was straightforward: if KPMG's internal process had failed this catastrophically, the processes of the other three firms — which had each had their own whistleblower controversies — were equally suspect. "ASIC probes big four – after gutting its own audit teams," headlined The Australian, capturing both the regulator's ambition and the structural weakness of the agency that was attempting it.

## What ASIC can and cannot do

ASIC's powers over registered company auditors are real but bounded. Every auditor of a major Australian company must be registered with ASIC as a "registered company auditor." That registration gives the regulator a lever no other body holds: it can discipline, suspend, or cancel an individual auditor's registration. For the partners involved in the Lendlease breach, that meant the possibility of deregistration — a sanction that would end their audit careers.

But the regulator's reach has limits. ASIC cannot easily reach into the partnership structure to hold the firm itself to account; the firm is not a registered auditor, only its individual partners are. ASIC's enforcement model is case-by-case, individual-by-individual — a model that is poorly suited to systemic cultural failure. And ASIC's budget for audit supervision had been cut, its audit review teams weakened, in the years before the scandal. The regulator investigating the biggest audit scandal in a generation was also, in the view of its critics, the regulator that had been stripped of the capacity to have prevented it.

## The three named auditors

The investigation centred on individual partners. Three KPMG partners had been sanctioned over the $32m Westpac audit pitch — the internal sanction that preceded the regulator's involvement. ASIC's probe, according to reporting, extended to those partners and to others involved in the Lendlease material. The regulator's focus on individuals reflected both its legal powers and its strategic judgment: deregistering a partner sends a message to the profession in a way that a fine on the firm might not.

The individuals' names have been reported in part, but the full detail of ASIC's allegations — and the evidence it has gathered — remains confidential. The parliamentary committee's demand for the internal investigation files, and ASIC's parallel power to compel documents under its own statutory powers, created a two-front battle over the same documents.

## The wider review

The wider ASIC review into the big four's complaint-handling was, in structural terms, the more significant development. It signalled that the regulator understood the KPMG affair not as the story of a few bad partners but as a systemic failure of the self-regulation model. If the review produces enforceable requirements — independent investigation of internal complaints, disclosure to regulators of complaints and outcomes, separation of hotline and investigation functions — it would be the most significant reform of the audit profession in decades.

The review's scope was confirmed by reporting: "ASIC launches sweeping review of Big Four audit firms' complaint handling after KPMG misconduct allegations" (finance.biggo.com); "ASIC probes big four firms after KPMG scandal" (grafa.com); "Australia regulator reviews audit conduct complaints at Big Four after KPMG scandal" (TradingView). By August, the review had grown: SmartCompany and Capital Brief reported that ASIC had expanded its probe into the big four audit firms after the KPMG allegations — a widening that landed in the same week as the parliamentary hearing, as the committee's evidence and the regulator's investigation converged on the same facts.

## August: a second front opens

The 14 August hearing opened a second regulatory front, this one driven by Senator Barbara Pocock's questions on notice. As The Australian reported on 19 August, those answers disclosed that ASIC is investigating KPMG entities for alleged breaches of Part 9.4AAA of the *Corporations Act 2001* — the statutory provision that governs whistleblower protections. Where the original audit probe targeted individual partners' conduct, the Part 9.4AAA investigation targets the firm's treatment of the person who reported it: the dismissal, the surveillance, and the settlement of a second, tax-division whistleblower for "larger than $500,000" under a confidentiality agreement. Breaching Part 9.4AAA carries civil penalties of up to 10 per cent of annual turnover for corporations, and personal civil fines exceeding $1 million plus up to two years' imprisonment for individuals who retaliate against or victimise whistleblowers — a regime whose teeth, as the committee noted, had never been tested at this scale.

The financial picture the hearing disclosed made the regulatory stakes concrete. The Australian reported on 19 August that KPMG had begun a "brutal cull" of 50 partners and was preparing to shed 450 further client-facing staff, with new chief executive John Sams confirming in a partner email that "conversations have commenced this week with impacted Partners." Clients and government departments were reconsidering some $442 million of contracts with the firm. In a further indication of the squeeze, The Australian reported the firm had gone "cap in hand to KPMG International seeking financing," with the revenue cliff landing on top of "record high personal borrowings accrued under former chief executive Andrew Yates." In a cruel twist, KPMG would not shed further staff from its audit or tax teams in the cull — those were the divisions the scandal had arisen from, but "it needs them on deck to retain clients."

## The limits of the regulator

For all its significance, the ASIC investigation embodies the limits of the system this book describes. ASIC can discipline individual auditors. It cannot easily dismantle the partnership structure, cannot easily hold a firm's culture to account, and is operating with resources that its own critics describe as insufficient to the task. The whistleblower's complaint took three years to reach the Parliament and the regulator. The structure that delayed it — the internal investigation, the privilege claims, the partnership's control of its own record — is the structure ASIC must now unpick.

The investigation's outcome will be one of the central stories of this book's later chapters. Whether ASIC's individual-partner model can produce justice proportionate to a systemic failure — or whether it will, as its critics predict, land on a few individuals while the structure remains intact — is the question that hangs over the entire affair.
