# Chapter 8 — The FairCall Hypocrisy

*Status: draft v0.2*

Of all the ironies in the KPMG affair, one was so stark it became a story of its own: the firm that dismissed and surveilled its own whistleblower had built a business selling whistleblower services to other companies. Under the brand FairCall, KPMG ran outsourced whistleblower hotlines for some of the most important institutions in Australia — the Reserve Bank, NAB, the ASX, and a hundred other organisations — promising the very protection it had denied its own employee.

The contradiction was not lost on anyone. "What scandal? KPMG continues to dish out whistleblower advice," wrote The Age at the end of May, as the firm's crisis management team continued to market the very service whose integrity the crisis had destroyed.

## Outsourced conscience as a service

The FairCall model was simple and, in its way, brilliant. Whistleblower hotlines are a legal and reputational necessity for large organisations, but building a credible internal reporting process is difficult, expensive, and — crucially — requires independence. KPMG's answer was to sell that independence: FairCall would run the hotline, receive the disclosures, and pass the investigation work to KPMG's own forensic and investigations teams. The client got a turnkey compliance solution. The auditor got access to the client's most sensitive disclosures.

The commercial logic was compelling. The RBA, NAB, ASX, and a long list of other clients signed on. FairCall became a standard part of KPMG's service offering — a line of business that traded, explicitly and profitably, on the firm's promise of integrity.

## The contradiction

The contradiction became unbearable once the Lendlease breach was exposed. KPMG's internal handling of its own whistleblower had been the exact opposite of what FairCall promised clients. The internal complaint had been investigated by the firm itself, dismissed, and the complainant had been dismissed and surveilled. The one thing FairCall sold — independent, confidential, protected reporting — was precisely what KPMG had failed to provide to its own people.

The Australian's verdict was blunt: "'Obscene': KPMG's whistleblower hotline under fire." The word "obscene" was used because the juxtaposition was so raw: the firm selling protection while its own protection failed so catastrophically.

## The clients respond

The response of clients was immediate and — for KPMG — commercially significant. The RBA announced it would review its FairCall contract. RBA Governor Michele Bullock confirmed the central bank would re-tender the whistleblower hotline contract amid the scandal. "RBA set to drop KPMG from whistleblower hotline as crisis deepens," reported the International Accounting Bulletin. NAB and other institutions began reviewing their arrangements. The ASX, whose own whistleblower line KPMG ran, faced the question of whether its market operator could be seen to rely on the very firm under investigation.

The client exodus from FairCall was not simply a reputational matter. It was a direct hit on a revenue stream — and a demonstration that the market could do what the regulators had failed to do: impose a cost on the firm's misconduct.

## ASIC's probe of the hotline line itself

The scandal also brought FairCall itself under scrutiny. ASIC's investigation into KPMG, and the regulator's wider review of how the big four handle internal complaints, extended to the whistleblower hotline business. The questions were structural: if KPMG's own complaint-handling had failed so badly, what confidence could clients have in the complaint-handling KPMG sold to them? Did the FairCall model — hotline run by KPMG, investigations by KPMG, advice by KPMG — provide the independence the service claimed?

The deeper question, which the parliamentary committee pursued, was whether the FairCall model had ever been independent at all. A whistleblower hotline run by the firm that audits the client is not independent in any meaningful sense. It is the audit conflict — the firm both policing and selling to the same client — rendered in miniature. The scandal exposed FairCall not as an isolated failure but as the logical product of the structure this book describes: the partnership selling integrity as a product while the integrity of its own house collapsed.

## The lesson

FairCall was the KPMG scandal in a single product. The firm's business model depended on trust — the trust of whistleblowers, of clients, and of the public — while its internal conduct destroyed that trust. When the RBA re-tendered the hotline, when clients began to leave, and when ASIC's review extended to the complaint-handling business, the message was the same one the whistleblower had tried to deliver three years earlier: the firm that sells integrity must practise it. And KPMG had not.

## The end of FairCall

In August 2026, the contradiction was resolved in the only way left: the service was withdrawn. Capital Brief reported on 18 August that KPMG had ceased providing the FairCall whistleblower hotline to its big-name audit clients; big4news reported on 23 August that KPMG would stop FairCall "for audit clients as whistleblower scandal clouds hotline business," and Going Concern noted the irony in its Friday Footnotes — the "firm in whistleblower scandal ends whistleblower service for clients." The firm that had sold independent reporting to half of corporate Australia could no longer sell it to anyone, because the product's premise — that KPMG could be trusted with disclosures — had been publicly destroyed by KPMG itself.

The withdrawal was framed as a commercial decision; it functioned as an admission. A firm confident that its own scandal was contained does not retire its flagship integrity product. FairCall's end marked the point at which the KPMG affair stopped being a story about one mishandled complaint and became a story about the market for integrity itself — and what happens to that market when the biggest seller is caught selling the opposite.
