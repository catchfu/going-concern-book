# Chapter 6 — The Hoggett Decision

*Status: draft v0.2*

Of all the decisions KPMG made in 2026, the one that most exposed the firm's character was not the original breach — it was the decision about how to punish the person accused of it. The internal investigation into Eileen Hoggett, the firm's Chief Operating Officer, had reached a conclusion. The conduct panel had made its findings. And then the board — the same board whose independent members had been appointed to provide oversight — overruled its own legal and HR executives to soften the penalty.

The story of the Hoggett decision is the story of how a firm, confronted with its own misconduct, chose institutional self-protection over the verdict of its own processes. It is, in microcosm, the original sin of Chapter 1.

## The demotion

The Australian broke the story on 4 June 2026: "KPMG board overruled legal, HR on disgraced COO penalty." The reporting established that the internal investigation into Hoggett had recommended a penalty significantly harsher than what the board ultimately imposed. The firm's legal advisers and its HR function had argued for one outcome; the board — led, according to reports, by the chairman — chose another. Hoggett was demoted. She did not leave the firm. For many inside and outside KPMG, the expectation had been that the COO — the executive whose division had mishandled the whistleblower's complaint and whose conduct was at the centre of the Lendlease breach — would be gone.

The board's decision had a logic, of course. A demotion avoided the disruption of losing a senior executive. It avoided the legal risk of a wrongful-dismissal claim. It kept the matter internal. But the decision was also, self-evidently, a decision about the firm's values: faced with a choice between the recommendation of its own processes and the convenience of the partnership, the board chose the partnership.

## The conduct panel's findings

The conduct panel had been constituted under the firm's internal disciplinary rules. Its findings — which have never been published — were, according to the reporting, sufficiently serious that the recommended penalty was dismissal or a penalty far beyond demotion. The exact nature of the panel's findings is one of the central unknowns of the affair, protected by the firm's claim of legal professional privilege.

What is known is that the board's overruling of the panel's recommendation was itself a decision that the whistleblower's lawyers and the parliamentary committee would seize on. If the firm's internal process could be overridden by the very people the process was meant to hold to account, then the process was not a protection for anyone — not for the whistleblower, not for the public, and not for the integrity of the audit function.

## "KPMG fails its own character test"

The Australian's verdict was devastating and precise: "KPMG fails its own character test on Hoggett." The phrase captured the deepest irony of the affair. KPMG's brand promise — the promise that sat atop every audit it performed — was that the firm stood for integrity, independence, and professional character. Its handling of Hoggett demonstrated the opposite: that when character collided with the interests of the partnership, the partnership won.

The decision also had direct commercial consequences. Dexus, one of KPMG's audit clients, had made clear that Hoggett was "a no-go" — that it would not accept the demoted COO on its audit. When KPMG's client relationships depend on keeping a disgraced executive away from the work, the firm's internal governance decisions become external commercial problems. Lendlease had already left; Westpac was reconsidering; and the board's protection of Hoggett was, in the view of clients, evidence that the firm had not understood the gravity of what it was accused of.

## The character of the decision

The Hoggett decision reveals the structure of power inside KPMG Australia. The board that overruled the conduct panel included the chairman, Martin Sheppard, whose own position was already under pressure. The decision protected a senior partner at the expense of the firm's stated values. And it was made in the teeth of the firm's own legal and HR advice — the very functions whose job it is to tell the partnership when it is wrong.

The significance of the decision extends beyond Hoggett herself. It demonstrates, in concrete form, the argument that runs through this book: that the partnership model, with its self-regulation and its protected internal processes, cannot be relied on to hold its own members accountable. When the firm's own conduct panel recommends one outcome and the board delivers another, the system has failed not because of a single bad actor but because the structure places the interests of the partnership above the integrity of the process.

## The aftermath

Hoggett's demotion did not save her, and it did not save the board. Within weeks of the decision, the chairman had resigned, the CEO was gone, and the firm was in the hands of a leadership overhaul. The demoted COO — the figure at the centre of the decision — became a symbol of everything the firm had got wrong: the protection of its own, the overruling of its own processes, and the cost of confusing institutional convenience with professional character.

## Canberra: Hoggett gives evidence

On Friday 14 August 2026, Hoggett appeared before the parliamentary inquiry in Canberra — no longer as the firm's COO but as a former executive, summoned to answer for the conduct this chapter has described. The Australian billed the session "KPMG Part Deux" and judged it "more tears, less blood at KPMG's Canberra sequel"; Accountants Daily reached for a different verdict, labelling Hoggett the "naughtiest kid in the class."

Her testimony reproduced, in miniature, the firm's pattern. Capital Brief reported that the former COO couldn't remember storing Lendlease documents in a locker — a failure of recollection about the physical handling of the very papers whose misuse had started the scandal. Her evidence sat alongside the AFR's report that a KPMG lawyer accused partners of lying to cover up the scandal: at the same hearing, the firm's own representatives were accusing one another. What the board had decided in private in June — the overruled panel, the softened penalty — was being re-litigated in public, and the board's leniency looked less like mercy than like delay.
