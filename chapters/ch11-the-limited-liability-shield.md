# Chapter 11 — The Limited Liability Shield

*Status: draft v0.1*

Behind the scandal's drama of resignations and parliamentary hearings sits a structure so arcane that it rarely surfaces in the news coverage, and so consequential that it determines whether the victims of the KPMG affair — the clients whose confidential material was leaked, the shareholders whose audits were corrupted — will ever see a cent of compensation. It is the legal architecture that protects the partners of the big four from the consequences of their firms' conduct: the limited liability partnership, and the statutory caps on professional liability that sit beneath it.

This chapter is more legal than narrative, because the stakes are legal. When the whistleblower's complaint was dismissed and surveilled, when Lendlease's board papers were leaked into rival pitches, and when confidential Optus information was misused, the question that followed each revelation was the same: who is liable, and how much can they be made to pay?

## The professional standards regime

Australia's professional liability regime is a product of the late 1990s and early 2000s, when a crisis in professional indemnity insurance threatened the viability of the professions. The solution was a bargain: the professions — including accountancy — would submit to statutory oversight of their professional standards, and in return, their members' liability would be capped. For accountants, the effect is that a firm's liability for professional negligence is limited — often dramatically — by the amount of insurance the firm is required to hold, rather than by the scale of the damage its negligence caused.

The mechanism is the professional standards legislation operating in each state, administered for the accountancy profession by CA ANZ. A firm that complies with the scheme's requirements — insurance, complaints handling, risk management — is entitled to have its members' liability capped. The cap is not zero; it scales with the size of the firm and the amount of insurance held. But for a firm like KPMG Australia, the effect is that even a catastrophic failure of the audit function exposes partners to a liability ceiling far below the market capitalisation of the firms they audit.

## The partnership structure

Beneath the statutory caps sits the partnership structure itself. KPMG Australia operates as a limited liability partnership, in which partners are shielded from liability for the negligence or misconduct of other partners. The whistleblower's complaint concerned the conduct of a partner using confidential documents in a rival pitch. Under the LLP structure, the firm's liability for that conduct is the firm's, not the partners' personal liability. The victims must sue the firm — and hit the caps — rather than the individuals responsible.

The design is deliberate. The LLP converts what was once a collective of professionals each personally exposed to the firm's errors into a corporate shell that protects each partner from everyone else's mistakes. It is, in the language of the profession, a way to make the firm a viable place to work — and, in the language of its critics, a way to make the firm's victims carry the risk that the profession cannot be bothered to manage.

## The Lyon lawsuit

The most direct challenge to the shield comes from Brendan Lyon, a University of Wollongong professor and former Treasury official who became a whistleblower in his own right in the Lendlease affair. Lyon's lawsuit against CA ANZ — the professional body that administers the liability caps — tests whether the professional standards regime can be used to protect the big four from accountability for conduct that is not merely negligent but deliberate. Lyon's argument, as reported, is that the statutory liability protections were designed for professional negligence, not for misconduct — that a firm that deliberately leaked confidential client information cannot shelter behind caps intended for honest error.

The case is being watched with an intensity the profession has rarely seen. If Lyon succeeds, the effect would be to pierce the limited liability shield in cases of deliberate misconduct — exposing partners to the full consequences of the firm's wrongdoing. If he fails, the shield stands, and the lesson of the KPMG affair is grim: the partnership structure, designed to protect professionals from honest mistakes, will have been shown to protect deliberate wrongdoing from its consequences.

## The tax dimension

The shield has a tax dimension that the ATO has pursued. Reporting revealed that big four partners split a significant share of their income through structures that minimised their tax exposure — reported figures of around 44 per cent of income were the subject of ATO scrutiny. The point for this chapter is not the tax avoidance itself but what it reveals: a partnership structure that is flexible enough to be used for the partners' financial convenience is also flexible enough to be used to shelter them from liability. The same architecture that lets the partners minimise their tax lets them cap their exposure to the firm's misconduct.

## What the shield means for the victims

The practical consequence of the limited liability shield is that the victims of the KPMG affair face a legal journey with a ceiling. Lendlease, Optus, Telstra, the shareholders of the companies whose audits were corrupted, and the whistleblower herself can sue — but their recovery is bounded by the caps and the insurance arrangements of a firm whose structure was designed, in part, to limit what it can be made to pay. The shield is the reason the accounting profession's scandals produce resignations and apologies but rarely compensation commensurate with the harm.

The reform debate — the break-up of the big four, the capping of partnerships, the removal of the professional standards caps for deliberate misconduct — is fundamentally a debate about this shield. When Senator Barbara Pocock and others call for structural separation of audit and consulting, they are not only attacking the conflict of interest; they are attacking the liability architecture that makes the conflict profitable. When the parliamentary committee demands the internal investigation files, it is fighting the other half of the shield: the privilege that keeps the record of misconduct private.

The shield has two layers — the statutory caps and the privilege protecting the files — and both have to fall before the KPMG affair produces the accountability its victims were promised.
