# Chapter 3 — The Whistleblower

*Status: draft v0.2*

The complaint was lodged internally, in the ordinary way that complaints at a big four firm are supposed to be lodged: quietly, formally, and with the expectation that the firm's own processes would take it seriously. The person who raised it was a former audit director at KPMG Australia. The person it concerned was, at the time, one of the most powerful figures in Australian audit — a senior executive whose conduct the complainant believed had crossed a line.

The complaint alleged that confidential client information had been misused — specifically, that KPMG audit partners had accessed and used confidential board papers belonging to the firm's audit client Lendlease, and that the breach involved not only Lendlease but, as the affair would later widen, the clients Optus and Telstra. It was, on its face, the most serious kind of allegation an auditor can make: not a grievance about pay or culture, but a claim that the firm's core function — the independent verification of company accounts — had been corrupted by the firm's own commercial ambitions.

KPMG's response, as the investigation would eventually show, was to investigate the complaint internally, find it wanting, and dismiss it. The complainant was dismissed from the firm. And then, in the detail that would define the entire affair, the firm's conduct went further: the whistleblower alleged that KPMG had surveilled her — that her laptop had been accessed, and that there had been attempts to track her communications. The firm would later confirm, under parliamentary questioning, that it had "surveilled" the whistleblower's laptop.

## The first investigation

The internal investigation into the complaint concluded that the allegations were not substantiated. The complainant was dismissed. Within the partnership, the matter was treated as closed. Nobody at board level was told, or so it would later be claimed. The investigation file — the record of what KPMG had found, and of how it had handled the finding — became a point of intense controversy. Parliamentary committees would eventually demand to see it, and KPMG would refuse, citing legal professional privilege.

The consequences of dismissing the complaint were felt almost immediately. The complainant did not accept the outcome. She took her allegations to journalists, and then — crucially — to the Commonwealth Parliament, where she made them under the protection of parliamentary privilege. Under that protection, she could say, without fear of defamation, what she had been unable to say internally. It was the first time the PwC playbook had been inverted: instead of a leaked document driving the scandal, it was a dismissed employee using the one channel the firm could not control.

## "Blows my mind"

In the last days of May 2026, the AFR and Michael West Media began publishing the whistleblower's account. The headline that captured the mood was from Michael West: "'Blows my mind': KPMG's whistleblower issues collide." The phrase referred to the fact that KPMG's problems were not one scandal but two colliding at once — the Lendlease breach and the surveillance of the whistleblower — and that the firm had managed to compound each with the other.

The speed of the fallout was extraordinary. On Friday 29 May 2026, KPMG Australia's CEO Andrew Yates resigned. The firm's audit head — the partner responsible for the very function at the centre of the allegations — also stepped down. Reuters, The Age, The Guardian, City AM, and the financial press worldwide carried the resignations within hours. The firm's own statements framed the departures as accountability for the handling of the complaint rather than as an admission of the underlying conduct. But the resignations did nothing to slow the investigation.

## The committee takes over

Senator Deborah O'Neill, chair of the parliamentary committee that had driven the PwC scandal, made the KPMG affair her next target. Her language was unmistakably personal: "I will not let this rest," she said, describing KPMG's response to the whistleblower's allegations. The committee began demanding documents and testimony. KPMG executives were summoned. The firm's response — that the internal investigation had been proper, that the complaint had been investigated and dismissed, that the firm had since changed its processes — was met with increasing disbelief.

The pivotal moment came when the committee learned, via the whistleblower's account and subsequent reporting, that KPMG had not only investigated and dismissed the complaint, but had surveilled the complainant's laptop. The revelation that the firm had tracked the very person who had tried to use its internal channels converted a corporate governance scandal into something else entirely: a story about power, surveillance, and the treatment of the person who tried to speak.

## The cost of disbelief

The decision to dismiss the complaint — and to investigate the complainant rather than the allegation — proved to be KPMG's defining error. It turned a potentially containable governance failure into a crisis that consumed the firm's leadership. By the end of June, the chairman had resigned too. Clients were leaving. ASIC had opened a formal investigation. The government had barred KPMG from new federal work. And the firm's own head of audit — the man whose division the complaint had concerned — was gone.

The whistleblower's story is the spine of this book, and it will recur in every subsequent chapter. But its essential lesson is simple: the firm that dismisses the messenger because the message is inconvenient, and then surveils the messenger to prove a point, has abandoned the very values its own marketing promises to clients.

## The August hearing

When the parliamentary inquiry held its hearing in Canberra on Friday 14 August 2026 — a session The Australian billed as "KPMG Part Deux" — the whistleblower's complaint was no longer the only matter before the committee. It was the spine of a hearing at which former KPMG executives were grilled over the firm's conduct, in the ABC's words, and which heard that the firm faced misconduct claims well beyond the original complaint. Reuters reported that the probe heard KPMG Australia was facing "many more" whistleblower misconduct claims; News.com.au counted "many, many more" claimants. The scandal that had begun with one dismissed audit director had, by the time of the hearing, become a stream of complaints the firm had still not contained.

The week before the hearing set the scene. On 10 August, The Australian reported that a law firm had cleared KPMG partners before all hell broke loose — the pre-scandal external validation the hearing would interrogate. The next day, Law.com reported that KPMG had withheld information from Ashurst and Perkins Coie in the whistleblower investigation — an allegation that, if accurate, meant even the firm's own external review had been fed a partial record. On 11 August, The Australian reported that the firm's general counsel and HR chief were quitting ahead of the whistleblower inquiry, and — in the days that followed — that KPMG executives had resigned with $2m payouts ahead of the inquiry.

The hearing also confirmed that the firm's whistleblower problem was not one complaint but at least two. The AFR reported on 12 August that KPMG had paid more than $500,000 to a whistleblower in its tax division — a separate whistleblower with separate allegations, settled by the same firm that had dismissed and surveilled its audit whistleblower. KPMG said the settlement "had no impact on investigations," according to Accounting Times. And on 16 August, two days after the hearing, The Australian reported that the whistleblower had won a ruling to attack the big four's secretive partner shield — the most concrete legal victory the whistleblower's cause had yet achieved, examined in Chapter 11.

## The Canberra testimony

The August hearing's most sustained exchange centered on the firm's own internal investigation. KPMG's deputy counsel, James McClelland — the lawyer who had run that investigation — appeared close to tears as he told the committee, "I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry I have been part of this. I am sorry," according to The Australian's live coverage of 14 August. McClelland then went further than any KPMG witness had the previous month: he said "the truth was not told in those interviews" by the partners he had spoken to, and asserted his investigation had been "fundamentally undermined" — "by the answers which were given, which were misleading, if not directly deceptive, to the questions I asked," and "by limitations of scope on the work I undertook."

Senator O'Neill pressed McClelland on an instruction he had reportedly given at the time the complaint first arose: that anyone who encountered the whistleblower should "run past him and ignore him," and that the whistleblower was "trying to throw mud at people." The exchange confirmed what the complaint stage had suggested — that the firm's own investigators had been told to treat the messenger as the threat, not the message.

The accounting of departures grew sharper in Canberra. "Eight people have left the firm," The Australian reported McClelland saying, "including chief executive Andrew Yates and Mr Sheppard," along with the audit head Julian McPherson and partners named under privilege. The departures now read as an almost complete turnover of the firm's pre-scandal leadership, with only the general counsel, Louise Capon, remaining — and she was to stand down within days.

## The new chair meets the whistleblower

The new chair, Michael Ebeid, told the committee he had held his first video call with the whistleblower only two days earlier, and described the effort it had taken to reach that point: "Over the past two years they have suffered enormously... It took me several weeks of emails to develop a level of trust with the whistleblower … but I am really really pleased that two days ago was the first time we organised a video meeting." Ebeid pledged his focus would be "what the whistleblower wants and what the whistleblower needs, and that is paramount," and volunteered to provide "restorative justice to the whistleblower" without putting a figure on it.</ Senator Scott Buchholz had proposed releasing the whistleblower from their deed of arrangement as a first step; Ebeid did not commit to a figure.

## The regulator responds

The Canberra hearing widened the scandal beyond politics. Senator Barbara Pocock, questioning former chair Martin Sheppard about a reported settlement with another whistleblower in the tax division, established that the amount was "larger than $500,000" — subject to a confidentiality agreement Sheppard declined to break. More consequentially, the same line of questioning surfaced an ASIC probe launched by way of answers to Pocock's questions on notice: ASIC is investigating KPMG entities for alleged breaches of Part 9.4AAA of the *Corporations Act 2001*, the statutory provision governing whistleblower protections. Breaching those provisions carries civil penalties of up to 10 per cent of annual turnover for companies, and personal fines exceeding $1 million plus up to two years' imprisonment for individuals who retaliate against or victimise whistleblowers. The Australian reported the August investigation on 19 August as part of its coverage of the firm's response to the scandal going public.
